"My website doesn't need rescuing. It works fine."

I hear this a lot, and usually it's true. The pages load. The pictures are there. Nobody has called to say anything is wrong.

But "it works" covers a lot of ground. A website can work perfectly well while costing more every year than it should, depending on a company that's quietly falling apart, or sitting in an account nobody can log into. None of that shows up when you open your own homepage.

When I say "rescue," I don't only mean pulling something out of the rubble. Sometimes it's that. More often it means taking a site that's fine and making it cheaper, simpler, and genuinely yours. And sometimes the honest answer is that nothing needs doing at all.

---

## What Are You Actually Paying For?

The most common rescue starts with a bill.

One artist I work with was paying a site builder about $330 a year for a handful of pages and a couple of domain names. Someone else had four small WordPress sites costing around $150 a year between them, and none of the four had changed in a long time.

These aren't outrageous numbers. That's exactly why they keep getting paid. The renewal arrives, it's not quite enough to be worth a fight, and another year goes by.

But most artists' and photographers' sites show the work, an about page, a CV and a way to get in touch, and change once or twice a year. A site like that doesn't need a database or a monthly subscription to a platform built for online stores. It needs to be fast, look right, and stay put.

I once [rebuilt a small site](/work/writing-sos/) with the same design and the same content, minus the hosted system behind it that nobody was using. Visitors couldn't tell the difference. The subscription simply stopped.

To be fair, Squarespace, Wix and WordPress are the right answer for plenty of people. If you update every week, sell through your site, or just love the editor, keep it. The question is only whether they fit a site that mostly sits still.

## When the Pictures Go Missing

The same artist with the $330 bill had a stranger problem too.

She has two web addresses. One is her real, current site. The other, the one printed in her email signature, turned out to be serving an old copy of the site from several years earlier. Its images pointed at files that had been deleted when the real site was updated. Anyone clicking the link in her emails landed on a portfolio full of empty boxes where the artwork should be.

Nothing was "down." Both sites loaded. From where she sat, everything worked.

Untangling that is detective work more than code: who controls each address, where each site lives, where the original files are. Then the part that matters most: making sure the artwork lives somewhere the owner controls, so it can't vanish again because a platform tidied up its storage.

## The Site That Went Away With the Domain

Sometimes the site really is gone.

A family member's portfolio, one of the first artist sites I ever looked after, disappeared when its domain expired. The hosting was tied to the domain, so when one lapsed, the other went with it. Nothing was hacked. A renewal was missed, which is how it usually goes: the notices go to an old email address, or a card expires, and the first sign of trouble is a friend saying, "I tried to look at your site and it's not there."

Even then, it's not always over. An embroidery artist I worked with had a site-builder portfolio that had been offline for years. Using public web archives and some digging, I found the original full-resolution photographs still sitting on the builder's own image servers, long after the site that used them had disappeared. [In that rescue](/work/alaina-varrone/), 138 artworks came back and went into a new portfolio she owns outright.

## Nobody Knows Who Has the Keys

Some rescues are mostly about confusion.

One artist was told that her web host had gone out of business. It hadn't. Before anyone checked, though, her domain had already been moved to a site builder with nothing built on it, and she'd been quoted $3,100 to recreate her site there.

What she actually needed was for someone to log into the old hosting account, see what was really there, and sort it out. Her site was restored and rebuilt, and her studio assistant now updates it herself.

This happens more often than you'd think. The designer has moved on, the domain is under someone's old email, and one login is on a sticky note. The site works, but nobody is sure who owns which piece of it.

## The Host That Kept Changing Hands

A conceptual artist came to me with a hand-built site he'd been running since the late 1990s: decades of work, made the way the early web was made, and that look was part of the art.

His longtime hosting company had been absorbed into a bigger provider. After that, support got harder to reach, the visitor statistics stopped working, and the server filled up with duplicate folders he didn't recognize. Another annual bill was coming. He didn't want a new website. He wanted something affordable, dependable, and easy to keep running.

[That's what he got](/work/nihilists-net/): the same site, still unmistakably itself, on secure hosting with no annual hosting fee, working statistics, and 36 redirects so old bookmarks still land in the right place. He edits his pages the same way he always has.

Notice what didn't happen: a redesign. Sometimes the best rescue changes nothing a visitor can see.

## Owning a Domain Isn't Owning a Website

Underneath all of these stories is the same question: who actually owns this thing?

Owning your domain isn't the same as controlling your website. The domain is the address; the website is the building. You can own the address outright and still have no way to get the building out of someone else's lot. A login to a site builder is the same trap with a friendlier face: you can edit your site all day, but you can't pick it up and take it anywhere. If you leave, you usually start over.

Hosting is different. Every website lives on someone's servers, and renting space on them is perfectly fine. What matters is whether you could move to different ones next week without rebuilding, because a website that works today isn't the same as one that can be maintained and moved tomorrow.

The way I build sites, the client owns all of it: the code repository, the source files, the content, the images, the domain, and the hosting account. Nothing is locked inside a proprietary builder. If you want to move to a different host, it can usually be done quickly, often in minutes, without rebuilding anything. And if you ever want someone else to look after it instead of me, you hand them everything.

I'd rather you keep working with me because it's going well, not because leaving would be painful.

## Your Website Doesn't Have to Be on Fire

So a rescue might mean recovering images and pages that have gone missing, rebuilding a site that disappeared with an expired domain, getting out of a subscription you no longer need, or untangling domains and logins so you know what you own. Or it might mean moving a perfectly good website to a cheaper, more dependable arrangement.

There's one more kind I'll write about separately: the site that looks good and works well, but that search engines and the people searching for you can barely see. That one is often fixed without touching the design at all.

Your website doesn't have to be on fire to need a rescue.
