"My website doesn't need rescuing. It works fine."

I hear this a lot, and usually it's true. The pages load. The pictures are there. Nobody has called to say anything is wrong.

But "it works" covers a lot of ground. A website can work perfectly well while costing more every year than it should, depending on a company that's quietly falling apart, sitting in an account nobody can log into, or being almost invisible to the people searching for it. None of that shows up when you open your own homepage.

When I say "rescue," I don't only mean pulling something out of the rubble. Sometimes it's that. More often it means taking a site that's fine and making it more affordable, more independent, easier to find, and easier to keep going for the next ten years.

And sometimes the honest answer is that nothing needs doing at all. I'll say so when that's the case.

---

## What Are You Actually Paying For?

The most common rescue starts with a bill.

One artist I work with was paying a site builder about $330 a year for a handful of pages and a couple of domain names. Another person had four small WordPress sites costing around $150 a year between them, and none of the four had changed in a long time. A writer I've been helping was paying roughly $240 a year across three hosted sites in plans, domains and email.

None of these are outrageous numbers. That's exactly why they keep getting paid. The renewal arrives, it's not quite enough to be worth a fight, and another year goes by.

But think about what a lot of artists' and photographers' sites actually do. They show the work. They have an about page, a CV, a way to get in touch. New pieces go up once or twice a year. A site like that doesn't need a database, a plugin ecosystem, or a monthly subscription to a platform that's built for online stores. It needs to be fast, look right, and stay put.

I once [rebuilt a small site](/work/writing-sos/) with the same design and the same content, minus the hosted system behind it that nobody was using. Visitors couldn't tell the difference. The subscription simply stopped.

I want to be fair here: Squarespace, Wix and WordPress are the right answer for plenty of people. If you're updating your site every week, selling things through it, or you just love the editor, keep it. The question isn't whether these platforms are bad. It's whether they're the right fit for a site that mostly sits still.

## When the Pictures Go Missing

The same artist with the $330 bill had a second problem, and it was a stranger one.

She has two web addresses. One is her real, current site. The other, the one printed in her email signature, turned out to be serving an old copy of the site from several years earlier. Its images pointed at files that had been deleted when the real site was updated. So anyone clicking the link in her emails landed on a version of her portfolio full of empty boxes where the artwork should be.

Nothing was "down." Both sites loaded. From where she sat, everything worked.

Untangling something like that is less about code and more about detective work. Who controls each address? Where is the current site actually hosted, and where is the old one? Where are the original image files, and which copies are the good ones? Then the part that matters most: making sure the artwork lives somewhere the owner controls, so it can't vanish again because a platform tidied up its storage.

## The Site That Went Away With the Domain

Sometimes the site really is gone.

A family member's portfolio, one of the first artist sites I ever looked after, disappeared when its domain expired. The hosting was tied to the domain, so when one lapsed, the other went with it. The site wasn't hacked or deleted. It just stopped existing because a renewal was missed.

That's the version of the story that tends to happen quietly. Renewal notices go to an old email address, or a credit card on file expires, and the first sign of trouble is a friend saying, "I tried to look at your site and it's not there."

Even then, it's not always over. An embroidery artist I worked with had a site-builder portfolio that had been offline for years. Using public web archives and some digging, I found that the original full-resolution photographs were still sitting on the builder's own image servers, long after the site that used them had disappeared. [In that rescue](/work/alaina-varrone/), 138 artworks came back and went into a new portfolio she owns outright.

## Nobody Knows Who Has the Keys

Some rescues are mostly about confusion.

One artist was told that her web host had gone out of business. It hadn't. Before anyone checked, though, her domain had already been moved to a site builder with nothing built on it, and she'd been quoted $3,100 to recreate her site there.

What she actually needed was for someone to log into the old hosting account, see what was really there, and sort it out. Her site was restored and rebuilt, and her studio assistant now updates it herself.

This happens more often than you'd think. The designer who built the site has moved on. The domain is registered under someone's old email. One login is on a sticky note, another is lost entirely. The site works, but nobody is quite sure who owns which piece of it. That's a rescue too, even if not a single page changes.

## The Host That Kept Changing Hands

A conceptual artist came to me with a hand-built site he'd been running since the late 1990s. It had decades of work on it, made the way the early web was made, and that look was part of the art.

His longtime hosting company had been absorbed into a bigger provider. After that, support got harder to reach, the visitor statistics stopped working, and the server filled up with duplicate folders he didn't recognize. Another annual bill was coming. He didn't want a new website. He wanted something affordable, dependable, and easy to keep running.

[That's what he got](/work/nihilists-net/): the same site, still unmistakably itself, moved to secure hosting with no annual hosting fee, working statistics, and 36 redirects so that old bookmarks still land in the right place. He edits his pages the same way he always has.

Notice what didn't happen: a redesign. Sometimes the best rescue changes nothing a visitor can see.

## Owning a Domain Isn't Owning a Website

Underneath all of these stories is the same question: who actually owns this thing?

Most people assume that if they pay for it, it's theirs. But there are a few distinctions that matter.

**Owning your domain isn't the same as controlling your website.** Your domain is the address. The website is the building. You can own the address outright and still have no way to get the building out of someone else's lot.

**Having a login to a site builder isn't the same as having your site.** You can edit it, but you can't pick it up and take it somewhere else. If you leave, you usually start over.

**Renting hosting is fine. Being locked into it isn't.** Every website lives on someone's servers. The question is whether you could move to different ones next week without rebuilding.

**A website that works today isn't the same as one that can be maintained and moved tomorrow.** The first is a good start. The second is the point.

The way I build sites, the client owns all of it: the code repository, the source files, the content, the images, the domain, and the hosting account. Nothing is locked inside a proprietary builder. If you want to move your site to a different host, it can usually be done quickly, often in minutes, without rebuilding anything. And if you ever want someone else to look after it instead of me, you hand them everything.

I'd rather you keep working with me because it's going well, not because leaving would be painful.

## Your Website Isn't Broken, It's Just Invisible

There's another kind of rescue that has nothing to do with bills or disappearing hosts. Everything works, the site looks good, and the owner is happy. But search engines can't make much sense of it.

The writer I mentioned earlier has a site that ranks first for the searches that matter most to it. By any normal measure, it's a success. Yet when we looked at what Google had actually indexed, 54 of its individual essays had been crawled and then left out. No sitemap had ever been submitted. The homepage's title and main heading needed fixing. A font file that nothing on the page used was loading on every visit. None of that was visible from the front door.

You'd never notice problems like these from looking at your own site. A few common ones:

**Pages that don't say what they are.** Every page titled "Home," or no description at all, so search results show whatever fragment the search engine guesses. Images with no text description, which means a search engine can't tell a painting from a placeholder, and neither can someone using a screen reader. Links to pages that moved years ago.

**Pages search engines can't reach.** A setting left over from development that tells search engines to stay away. No map of the site for them to follow. Two addresses for the same page, so neither one gets full credit.

**Missing context.** People can tell, glancing at a page, that this is an artist, that these are paintings, and that this is a list of exhibitions. Machines need to be told. There's a standard way to label that information, called structured data, that spells out who you are, what your works are, where you've shown, and how the pages relate.

**AI tools that summarize the web.** More and more people find artists and organizations by asking an AI assistant rather than typing into a search box. Nobody can promise that any particular site will be mentioned in those answers, and you should be wary of anyone who does. But a site with clear writing, a sensible structure, and well-labeled information is easier for these systems to read and to cite correctly.

**Bad social previews.** A site can look great until someone shares a link to your opening and the preview shows the wrong picture, a cropped logo, or nothing at all. Behind the scenes, a few lines of hidden information decide which image and description appear when your link is posted. Every page on this site has its own preview card for that reason.

**Slow, heavy, hard to use.** Photographs uploaded straight from the camera, so a gallery page weighs as much as a small app. Layouts that fall apart on a phone. Light gray text on white. Menus that can't be used without a mouse. These turn away visitors who never tell you they left.

**No idea who's visiting.** You should be able to see, simply, whether people are finding your site, how they got there, and which pages they look at. That doesn't require invasive tracking, cookie banners, or an expensive analytics plan.

For artists, the point isn't ranking for some generic phrase like "contemporary painter." It's making sure that when someone searches for your name, a specific piece, a past exhibition, or a project they heard about, they find you, and what they find is accurate.

And none of this has to change how your site looks. Some of the most valuable work happens entirely behind the scenes.

## Your Website Doesn't Have to Be on Fire

So what does a website rescue look like? It might mean:

- recovering images and pages that have gone missing
- rebuilding a site that disappeared with an expired domain or a closed account
- getting out of a subscription you no longer need
- untangling domains, logins and hosting accounts so you know what you own
- making your work easier to find in search and to share
- or simply moving a perfectly good website to a cheaper, more dependable arrangement

Most websites don't need to be torn down and rebuilt. Many just need someone to look at them carefully and fix the things nobody can see.

Your website doesn't have to be on fire to need a rescue.
