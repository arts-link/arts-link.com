Picture a website that looks good, loads, and makes its owner happy. Anyone who already knows the address can find everything on it.

And for a lot of the people who might be looking for it, it's practically invisible.

That's not a contradiction. What you see when you open your own site and what a search engine sees when it visits are two different things. A site can be beautiful to people and confusing to machines, and these days, machines decide a lot about who finds you.

I wrote recently about [websites that need rescuing](/blog/website-rescue/) even though they work fine: the surprise bills, the lapsed domains, the hosts that change hands. This is a different problem. Nothing is going wrong behind the scenes. Something just isn't happening.

---

## The Site That Ranks First and Still Goes Missing

A writer I've been working with has a site that ranks first in search for the phrases that matter most to it. By any normal measure, it's a success.

Then we looked at what Google had actually done with it. Of the site's individual essays, 54 had been visited by Google's crawler and then left out of the index, which means they couldn't turn up in search results at all. No sitemap had ever been submitted to tell the search engine what was there. The homepage's title and main heading needed fixing. And a 418 KB font file that nothing on the page used was loading anyway.

None of that was visible from the front door. The site looked exactly as it should.

I can't tell you yet what fixing them does for that site; the rebuild is new, and nobody can promise rankings anyway. What I can say is that each of these was a specific, checkable problem with a specific fix, and none of them required changing what the site looks like.

## Does Every Page Say What It Is?

Most of the problems I find are surprisingly ordinary.

Every page has a title that shows up in the browser tab and as the blue link in search results. On a lot of sites, every page is titled "Home," or the artist's name and nothing else. Each page also gets a short description, the gray text under that link. When there isn't one, the search engine grabs whatever fragment it likes, which is how a portfolio can end up described by its cookie notice.

Headings matter too. A page should have one main heading that says what it's about, and sections under it that make sense in order. People skim that way, and so do search engines.

And images need words. Every picture can carry a short text description, called alt text, that tells a search engine and anyone using a screen reader what's in it. Without it, a painting and a placeholder look the same. For an artist, whose whole site is pictures, that's a lot of invisible work.

## Can Search Engines Get In?

Sometimes the problem is more basic: search engines can't reach the pages at all.

A setting left over from when the site was being built tells search engines to stay away. There's no sitemap, the simple list of pages that helps them find everything. The same page lives at two or three slightly different addresses, so the credit gets split between them instead of going to one. Pages moved years ago, and the old links lead nowhere.

These are fixable, and they're the kind of thing worth checking before anything else. When I moved [a hand-built site that had been online since the late 1990s](/work/nihilists-net/), part of the job was 36 permanent redirects, so that addresses people had bookmarked and linked to for decades still land somewhere useful.

## Telling Machines Who You Are

When you look at an artist's site, you can tell in a second that this is an artist, these are their works, this is a list of exhibitions, and that's a contact page. A search engine has to work that out from the code.

There's a standard way to spell it out, called structured data. It's a small block of labeled information on each page, invisible to visitors, that says plainly: this is a person, this is their name, this is an artwork, this is a performance on this date at this place, this page belongs to that collection. This site uses it to describe the studio and its services.

It doesn't change how anything looks. It just removes the guesswork.

## What About AI Search?

More and more people find artists, writers and organizations by asking an AI assistant a question instead of typing into a search box. That raises a reasonable worry: is my work part of those answers?

I'll be straight about this. Nobody controls what those systems include, and anyone promising to get you "into" AI answers is promising something they can't deliver.

What does help is the same groundwork that helps search: clear writing that says who you are and what you make, pages that each have one job, accurate titles and descriptions, and labeled information about your work. A site like that is easier for any system, human or otherwise, to read and to quote correctly. That's worth doing for its own sake, and it doesn't depend on guessing where AI search goes next.

## The Wrong Picture in the Preview

Here's one most people only notice when it's embarrassing. Someone shares a link to your show, and the preview that appears in their post or message shows the wrong image, a stretched logo, or nothing at all.

That preview is controlled by a few lines of hidden information on each page that say which image, title and description to use when the link is shared. It's often left at whatever the platform defaults to.

On the sites I've built recently, every page gets its own preview image. When I rebuilt [an embroidery artist's portfolio](/work/alaina-varrone/), every piece of art got its own permanent page and its own preview card, so sharing a single work shows that work.

## Fast, Readable, and Usable on a Phone

A gallery page with photographs uploaded straight from a camera can weigh as much as a small app. On a good connection it's just slow. On a phone in a gallery basement, it never finishes loading.

The same goes for things that keep people out without anyone noticing: light gray text on white, layouts that fall apart on a small screen, menus that only work with a mouse. Search engines pay attention to speed and mobile layouts, but the bigger reason to fix them is the visitors who leave and never tell you why.

That embroidery portfolio scores 100 on Google's desktop Lighthouse test, a common measure of speed and good practice. Scores aren't the goal, but they're a useful check that nothing is getting in the way.

---

## Knowing Whether Anyone Finds You

Finally, you should be able to answer a simple question: are people finding my site, how, and what do they look at?

That doesn't have to mean invasive tracking or a cookie banner. On recent sites I've set up visitor statistics that don't use cookies at all. The trade-off is that they can't recognize a returning visitor, which is why it's your decision, not mine. Paired with Google's free Search Console, which shows the searches your site appears for, that's usually enough to see what's working.

---

## You Don't Need a New Website for This

The common thread: almost none of this requires a redesign. It's titles and descriptions, image text, a sitemap, redirects, labeled information, preview images, lighter files and a sensible way to count visitors. Most of it happens where visitors never look.

For artists, the point isn't to rank for some generic phrase like "contemporary painter." It's that when someone searches for your name, a particular piece, a past exhibition, or a project they heard about, they find you, and what they find is accurate.
